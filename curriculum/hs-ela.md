## Public Speaking: Opening, middle, close
### Structure
A good talk has an opening that grabs attention, a middle with two or three clear points, and a close that tells people what to remember or do.
### Delivery
- Speak slower than feels natural.
- Pause instead of saying um.
- Look at people, not at your notes.
- Use your hands to point, count and show size.
## Journalism: Who, what, where, when, why
### The five Ws
A news story answers who, what, where, when and why (and how) in its first paragraph.
### Checking sources
- Find where the claim started.
- Look for two independent sources.
- Check the date, the author and what they gain.

@lesson Public Speaking: Opening, middle, close
blurb: Build and deliver a short talk.
intro: Welcome to Public Speaking. Nearly everyone is nervous. Today you learn to turn that energy into a good talk.
points:
- Open with a hook: a question, a story or a surprising fact
- Make two or three clear points in the middle
- Close with the one thing you want people to remember
- Slow down, pause, and look at your audience
examples:
- Hook: "Did you know your brain uses about as much power as a light bulb?"
- Three points beat ten points
- A pause feels longer to you than to your audience
script:
- say[smile,wave]: Welcome to Public Speaking. I get nervous too, and I do this for a living. Nerves are just energy.
- board[left] A talk in three parts: Open: hook them | Middle: two or three points | Close: one thing to remember
- say[explain]: Pretend I'm giving a talk about sleep. I'd open with a question: when did you last feel fully rested?
- ask
- say[joy]: That pause was the technique. Silence makes people lean in.
- read: Public Speaking: Opening, middle, close/Delivery
check: order Put the parts of the talk in order.
- Open with a hook
- Make point one
- Make point two
- Close with the one thing to remember
glossary:
- hook: An opening that grabs the audience's attention.
- pace: How fast you speak.
- audience: The people listening to you.
whys:
- A clear structure helps listeners follow and remember.
- Slowing down makes you sound confident and helps people keep up.
wrap: Hook, two or three points, a strong close, and a slow, calm voice.

@lesson Journalism: Who, what, where, when, why
blurb: Write a news story and check your sources.
intro: Welcome to Journalism. Today we learn how reporters write a news story and decide what to trust.
points:
- The lead answers who, what, where, when and why
- Put the most important facts first (the inverted pyramid)
- Facts go in the story, opinions go on the opinion page
- Check every claim with two independent sources
examples:
- Lead: "Lincoln High's robotics team won the state title on Saturday in Columbus, after a tie-breaker round."
- Fact: the team scored 94 points. Opinion: the team is the best ever.
- A post with no source and no date is a reason to be careful
script:
- say[smile]: Welcome to Journalism. A free press depends on people who ask good questions and check the answers.
- board[left] The five Ws: Who | What | Where | When | Why (and how)
- say[explain]: Your first paragraph must answer all of them. If readers stop after one paragraph, they should still know what happened.
- ask
- say[stern]: And before you publish, check. Where did the claim come from? Do two independent sources agree? A fast mistake is worse than a slow truth.
- read: Journalism: Who, what, where, when, why/Checking sources
check: sort Fact or opinion?
- Fact: The game ended 3 to 2 | The library opens at 9 | The town has 4,000 people
- Opinion: It was the best game ever | The library should open earlier | The town is boring
glossary:
- lead: The opening of a news story that gives the key facts.
- source: A person or document that information comes from.
- bias: A leaning toward one side that can color how a story is told.
whys:
- Putting key facts first respects readers who only read the start.
- Two independent sources protect you from one person's mistake.
wrap: Answer the five Ws, put the facts first, and check your sources.
homework: Write a three-sentence news lead about something that happened at your school this week.
